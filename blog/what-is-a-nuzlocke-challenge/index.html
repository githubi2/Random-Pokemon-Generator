What Is a Nuzlocke Challenge?

A Nuzlocke is the reason a Bidoof on Route 2 can feel scarier than the final boss. It's the most popular way to replay Pokemon — a self-imposed ruleset that's been around for about fifteen years — and this guide covers where it came from, the rules people actually play with, and how to keep a first run alive past the third badge.

A Nuzlocke challenge is a self-imposed set of rules that turns any mainline Pokemon game into a permadeath run — beginner guides across the web, like Game8's challenge coverage, cover the same ground. No ROM hacks, no mods — you play the same game you already own, but every encounter matters and every faint is permanent.

Pokemon is designed so kids can finish it, so the difficulty ceiling is low. A Nuzlocke doesn't raise the ceiling; it raises the stakes. Losing a Pokemon you named and trained for ten hours is a different emotional experience from watching a random party member go down, and that difference is the entire format.

Where the Nuzlocke Challenge Came From

The format started in 2010, when a player named Nick Franco posted a challenge run of Pokemon Ruby as a comic strip — Bulbapedia keeps the full history — — "Pokemon: Hard Mode" — on 4chan's /v/ board. He drew one of his catches, a Nuzleaf, with the unimpressed face of John Locke from Lost, and readers fused the two names into "Nuzlocke." The comic's run ended in a team wipe; the ruleset outlived it. Within a few years the format was everywhere — forum logs, YouTube series, streamer marathons, and eventually dedicated tools like the Nuzlocke Team Builder on this site — and the core rules have barely changed since, a rare case of the internet agreeing on something and sticking with it.

The Three Core Nuzlocke Rules

Variants exist for everything, but the format rests on three rules. First: you may only catch the first Pokemon you encounter in each area. Route 3 gives you a Pidgey, your Route 3 catch is a Pidgey — if it breaks out, faints, or flees, you get nothing from that area. Second: if a Pokemon faints, it's dead. Not "revive it later" — dead, boxed forever in a graveyard box or released. Those two rules together rebuild the game. You stop picking favorites and start building around whatever the routes dealt you, and a random crit stops being an annoyance and starts being a funeral.

Third rule: nickname everything you catch. You'll understand why after the first death.

Edge cases get their own house rules, and the community mostly agrees on them: gift Pokemon, static encounters and your starter usually don't count against a route's first encounter, and fishing counts as its own encounter type on water routes — the house-rule debates have run for years on Nuzlocke Forums. Decide all of this before you start, not mid-run when the answer is convenient.

Optional Clauses Everyone Argues About

Almost nobody plays with just the core three. The community has standardized a menu of add-ons, and picking yours is part of setting up a run:

Dupes clause. If your first encounter is a species you already own, you may skip it and take the next thing. Nearly universal — without it, late-game routes full of Pidgey become dead zones.

Shiny clause. A shiny may be caught regardless of the first-encounter rule. Also nearly universal, because nobody on earth is walking past a shiny out of rule loyalty. (If you're curious how rare that moment actually is, we broke down the real math in Pokemon Shiny Odds Explained.)

Set mode. Battle style locked to Set, so the game doesn't tell you what the opponent is sending out next. This is the big difficulty spike and the standard for "serious" runs.

No items in battle. No potions, no X-items, no revives (which you can't use anyway). Popular with veterans; brutal for a first run.

Level cap. Your Pokemon may not exceed the next gym leader's ace level, which kills the classic strategy of just overleveling your starter through everything.

For a first run: core three plus dupes and shiny clauses. Add set mode and level caps once you've finished one.

Beginner Tips for Surviving Your First Nuzlocke

Most Nuzlocke deaths are preparation failures. Grind before every gym — in a normal playthrough grinding is optional, here it's the insurance policy, and ten extra minutes on a route is cheaper than a funeral. Then look up the boss before you walk in — Serebii's walkthrough data gives you the leader's ace, its level, and its coverage moves. Runs die to surprises far more often than to fair fights. Ask a room of players what ended their first Gen 2 run and Whitney's Miltank comes up more than anything else (the r/nuzlocke subreddit is a century of these stories) — that's community lore rather than a measured statistic, but twenty-odd years of forum threads make the point.

Overvalue the boring Pokemon. The starter feels like the star, but the things that keep runs alive are unglamorous: Normal-types with huge TM movepools, bulky walls, anything with a reliable status move — PokemonDB makes these quick to scout per route. Raticate in FireRed is the classic case — nobody's favorite, yet with Hyper Fang, Dig for Lt. Surge and a coverage TM it answers half the game. Plenty of winning teams are five ordinary route Pokemon and one starter who learned to share the spotlight.

Learn the sacrificial switch. When a Pokemon is about to eat a move it can't survive, switching in something expendable to take the hit is often the only play — "pivot fodder" is a real role on veteran teams, discussed at length in the Smogon community, and a planned death beats a random one. And when someone does die, keep playing. The run isn't over when you lose your favorite; it's over when you wipe. Some of the best Nuzlocke stories start with a death that forced a weird box Pokemon onto the team — and that Pokemon carrying the rest of the game.

On that note: agree with yourself in advance what a wipe means. Most players treat a full party wipe as the end of the run, full stop — new save file, new attempt. Others allow a "second chance" rebuild from the box in the post-game. Both are legitimate, but the first wipe is the moment most first-timers quietly quit, and having the rule written down beforehand makes the decision for you.

Start a Run With a Random Team

To feel the format's core skill without the 40-hour commitment, the Nuzlocke Team Builder deals you a random squad from the full dex — lock the team, forbid yourself rerolls, and you'll be practicing exactly what a real run demands: building around what you're given instead of what you wanted. You can even simulate whole challenge battles on Pokemon Showdown before committing to a cartridge file. For the real thing, game choice matters — Pokemon.com keeps the official game library for reference. FireRed and HeartGold are the classic beginner picks: small regional rosters, heavily documented boss fights, few nasty surprises. Skip Sword and Shield for a first attempt — the forced Exp Share smooths away the difficulty curve the challenge is supposed to create. Then nickname everything, and try not to get attached.
