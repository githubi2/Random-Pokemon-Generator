Pokemon Natures, Explained

You know the feeling. You finally get the Pokémon you've been after — the shiny, or the near-perfect IVs, or the one that took a whole afternoon of resets. Then you check the summary screen and there it is, plain as day: Rash. The one nature you didn't want, on the one Pokémon where it matters most. This guide walks the full 25-nature chart and shows how to actually pick one.

Here's the good news: natures have been around since Ruby and Sapphire, and the system hasn't moved an inch since. Same 25 natures, same math, same chart. That means the knowledge never goes stale. Twenty of those natures boost one stat by 10% and drop another by 10%. Five of them — Hardy, Docile, Serious, Bashful and Quirky — are neutral and change nothing at all. The boost and the drop never touch HP, only Attack, Defense, Sp. Atk, Sp. Def and Speed. The full breakdown is up on the Pokemon Nature Chart, and if you've never actually sat down with it, you're leaving real performance on the table.

What a 10% Boost Actually Means

A 10% swing sounds small until you see it in numbers. The modifier gets applied after the rest of the stat formula, so on a fully trained level-50 Pokémon you're looking at roughly 15 to 20 points in each direction. That's a 30 to 40 point gap between a good nature and a bad one. At level 100 the same 10% is worth closer to 30 points on a maxed stat. At competitive levels that gap decides who moves first and whether a hit knocks out. People will obsess over a single EV point and then shrug at nature, which is completely backwards. PokemonDB's mechanics page lays out the exact formula and the stat-by-stat spread for every nature, in case you want the numbers verified rather than quoted.

Natures also quietly decide which flavors a Pokémon likes and dislikes, a detail that shows up in things like curry making in Sword and Shield. It doesn't matter for battle, but it's a reminder that natures were designed as personality, not just math.

The First Rule: Drop What You Never Use

Picking a nature starts with an embarrassingly simple rule: drop the stat the Pokémon never touches. A physical attacker has no business investing in Sp. Atk, so it wants Adamant (+Attack, −Sp. Atk) or Jolly (+Speed, −Sp. Atk). Special attackers run the mirror image with Modest and Timid, which drop Attack instead. When the dropped stat is one the Pokémon never uses, the boost is pure profit. It costs you nothing. That single habit fixes more teams than any other piece of advice on the chart.

Garchomp is the textbook case. Base 130 Attack, base 80 Sp. Atk, base 102 Speed. It hits like a truck with physical moves and basically never touches its special movepool. Jolly is the standard choice: the speed boost lets it outrun the unboosted base-100 speed tier, and the drop lands on Sp. Atk, which it wasn't using anyway. Adamant is the common alternative on teams that already control speed with Tailwind, Sticky Web or priority moves, trading the speed edge for noticeably harder hits. The chart's FAQ walks through this exact example if you want the reasoning spelled out, and Smogon's Garchomp dex page confirms the standard sets and why Jolly wins the competitive vote.

Speed or Power?

Once the basics are covered, the real debate starts: speed or power. Jolly versus Adamant, Timid versus Modest, it's the argument every competitive player has had a hundred times. Speed natures win when they flip a specific matchup. A fast attacker that moves first can strike and never take the hit at all. Power natures win when the team already owns the speed game, or when the extra damage turns a two-hit knockout into a one-hit knockout. My rule of thumb, and the chart agrees: when in doubt on a fast attacker, take the Speed nature. Ties and near-ties are everywhere in the meta, and winning the speed tie is free value. If you want to see how much the meta actually rewards Speed, the usage statistics on r/stunfisk will show you a hundred sets that live or die on that tie.

Walls and Trick Room

Defensive Pokémon flip the whole thing around. Walls boost whichever defense is stronger. Impish and Careful are for walls that still throw out their Attack stat. Bold and Calm are for supporters that can drop Attack entirely. Since no nature touches HP, boosting a defense is the only way a nature adds bulk. A wall sitting on a neutral nature is wasting its only source of extra survivability.

Then there's Trick Room, which turns the chart upside down. Under Trick Room the slowest Pokémon moves first, so slow attackers actively want Brave (+Attack, −Speed) or Quiet (+Sp. Atk, −Speed). A base-20-Speed Pokémon with a Brave nature and minimized Speed IVs is one of the scariest things in the format while the room is up. Relaxed and Sassy do the same job for defensive Trick Room pieces. This is why competitive players get genuinely excited about Pokémon that look slow and useless on paper. The Bulbapedia nature article covers the full history and mechanics of the system, if you want the definitive reference behind every claim here.

Want to see all 25 natures at once instead of memorizing the four you use most? The Pokemon Nature Chart page has the full grid with every boost and drop in one table, plus a finder that reads a Pokémon's base stats and suggests the nature for it.

Caught the Wrong Nature? Fix It

Caught the perfect Pokémon with the wrong nature? Since Sword and Shield, you don't have to start over. Mints rewrite the stat effect to match any nature you want. A Jolly Mint makes the Pokémon behave exactly as if it were Jolly, even though the summary screen still shows the original nature. Sword and Shield sold them for 50 BP at the Battle Tower, and Scarlet and Violet mints come from the Battle Point shop and Tera Raid rewards — either way, a wrong nature is a fixable problem, not a lost Pokémon. The mint system is one of the few genuinely player-friendly additions in recent games, and Pokemon.com is the place to check the official game details if you are unsure what your copy supports.

So the loop is simple: catch or breed the Pokémon, check the nature, and if it's wrong either use it as-is (if the drop is harmless), reroll the hunt, or buy the mint. Every one of those decisions gets a lot easier when the whole chart is in front of you instead of in your head.
